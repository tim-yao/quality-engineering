# Testing Distributed Systems Across Delivery Teams

## Executive summary

Large software systems are often delivered by multiple teams that own separate applications, services, data pipelines and platforms. Every team can report green tests while a critical customer journey fails across team boundaries. This is not necessarily a lack of tests: it is often a failure of **system-level ownership, interface assurance, integration readiness and shared feedback**.

**Recommendation: use a federated quality engineering operating model.** Delivery teams own quality and verification of their changes; a small central or cross-cutting QE capability establishes minimum controls, makes systemic risk visible, enables shared tooling and coordinates assurance for critical cross-team flows. A named system or value-stream owner remains accountable for the end-to-end outcome.

This is a **proposed operating model**, not a formally standardised industry framework or an empirically proven universal optimum. The fit depends on service coupling, team maturity, release dependencies, regulatory constraints and organisational authority.

The intended end state is not a large central QA phase. It is a system in which teams can verify and deploy independently for routine changes, while genuinely cross-service risks receive explicit ownership and evidence-based assessment.

## The failure mode

Consider six delivery teams responsible for a frontend, identity service, business APIs, ETL, third-party integrations and cloud platform. Each can demonstrate local component readiness. Yet an end-to-end transaction might still fail because:

- providers and consumers disagree about field meaning or backward compatibility;
- asynchronously delivered events are duplicated or reordered;
- authorisation context is not carried across boundaries;
- a shared environment has incompatible versions, inaccessible dependencies or unsuitable data;
- failure recovery, observability and rollback were never validated as a whole.

A programme that waits until a late SIT window to find these problems has converted integration testing into its first effective integration control.

### Root causes, not just symptoms

| Level | Typical cause | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Technical | Contracts, message semantics, versions and failure modes are weakly specified or verified | Locally correct services fail together |
| Delivery process | Teams integrate late; SIT entry is assumed from deployment success | Slow defect discovery and expensive rework |
| Ownership | Nobody owns an entire customer journey or cross-team dependency | Risks fall between delivery backlogs |
| Organisation | Teams are rewarded for local completion dates rather than usable system outcomes | Optimisation of local green status |
| Feedback | Test results, traces, incident data and dependency status are fragmented | System-level readiness cannot be demonstrated |

DORA's guidance on [loosely coupled teams](https://dora.dev/capabilities/loosely-coupled-teams/) explicitly links effective delivery to teams being able to test and deploy on demand, without requiring extensive coordination or a common integrated environment. This is both an **architecture problem** and an operating-model problem.

## Compare the operating models

| Model | Strength | Failure mode | Appropriate context |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Centralised QA | Consistent oversight and specialist capacity | Handoffs, late feedback, QA becomes delivery gatekeeper | Temporary migration or highly constrained legacy delivery |
| Fully embedded QE | Fast feedback and close engineering collaboration | Duplication and cross-team assurance gaps | Small, loosely coupled services with clear owners |
| **Federated QE** | Team ownership plus system-wide assurance and enabling capability | Requires clear decision rights; central layer can become bureaucratic | Multiple teams sharing critical business journeys or platforms |

A federated model is not a reporting hierarchy in which one Principal QE approves all tests. It is a distributed ownership arrangement with targeted coordination for cross-boundary risks. Team Topologies is a useful conceptual basis for separating stream-aligned delivery, platform services and enabling capabilities; it does not itself prescribe the QE model in this article. See [Team Topologies — Key Concepts](https://teamtopologies.com/key-concepts/).

## Recommended responsibility model

| Quality activity | Accountable owner | Central / cross-cutting QE contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unit, component and local service tests | Owning engineering team | Guidance, minimum expectations, coaching |
| API and event contract compatibility | Provider and consumer teams, with named interface owner | Common tooling and visibility of incompatible versions |
| Cross-service integration scenarios | Named flow owner and participating teams | Risk mapping, coordination and evidence synthesis |
| Critical end-to-end journeys | Business/system owner with participating teams | Risk-based suite strategy and reporting |
| Performance, security and resilience | System/service owner with relevant specialists | Cross-cutting strategy and assurance |
| Environment and test data readiness | Platform/environment owner; data owners | Define testability requirements and expose blockers |
| Production quality and reliability | Service owner and operations/SRE | Connect incidents and reliability signals to engineering controls |
| Release decision and accepted residual risk | Authorised delivery/product/release decision-maker | Independent assessment of evidence and quality risk |

**Decision-right distinction:** QE assesses test coverage, readiness and residual quality risk; delivery and product leadership decide priorities, release timing and risk acceptance within organisational policy. A QA lead should not become accountable for software that other teams have not completed.

A useful rule is: every critical cross-team journey must have one **directly responsible individual (DRI)** for coordinating its verification, with named provider and consumer owners for each boundary. Coordination accountability does not eliminate each team's technical responsibility.

## Verification architecture: test at the cheapest trustworthy boundary

The UK Home Office's [Quality assurance and testing guidance, updated July 2025](https://engineering.homeoffice.gov.uk/principles/quality-assurance-and-testing/), recommends weighting component and API integration tests more heavily than UI-driven end-to-end tests where architecture allows, embedding resilience, accessibility and security, and using only targeted E2E coverage.

| Layer | Main question | Typical execution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unit / domain | Is the business logic correct? | Fast checks on each change |
| Component / local integration | Does the service behave with its database, queues or adapters? | Team-owned CI, reproducible dependencies |
| Contract / compatibility | Can released provider and consumer versions coexist? | Both sides' pipelines; version-aware compatibility checks |
| Cross-service integration | Do real boundaries, messages, data and failure recovery work? | Continuous, risk-selected integrated verification |
| Critical business E2E | Can a user complete a valuable business outcome across the system? | Small, resilient, production-like journey suite |
| Non-functional / operational | Does the system meet reliability, performance, security and recovery objectives? | Component and whole-system checks, including production feedback |

**Do not infer a fixed numerical test pyramid ratio.** Choose verification at the lowest layer that can faithfully expose the risk.

For example, a consumer-driven contract can detect a removed API field before deployment. It does **not** prove the business semantics of an ETL transformation, distributed transaction recovery or the correctness of an entire customer journey. These need additional targeted tests.

Pact's [can-i-deploy](https://docs.pact.io/pact_broker/can_i_deploy) illustrates version-aware deployment compatibility checks. It is a product-specific implementation example, not evidence that every architecture needs Pact.

For asynchronous services, prioritise idempotency, retries, ordering assumptions, dead-letter handling, eventual consistency and reconciliation. For shared auth and data flows, prioritise propagation of identity, permissions, data lineage and negative cases.

## Three lightweight cross-team controls

### 1. Dependency and interface ownership map

For each *critical or changing* integration, record provider, consumer, interface owner, contract, deployed versions, data semantics, known dependencies, validation evidence and escalation route.

Use the map to identify unowned edges and high-risk changes; avoid creating a complete inventory solely for governance.

### 2. Evidence-based integration readiness

An integrated environment being available does not mean a candidate is ready for formal cross-system testing.

Minimum expectations might include:

- required component scope and version identifiers are known;
- local business-rule, component and contract checks pass;
- dependent endpoints and necessary data are available;
- deployment and smoke checks pass;
- logs and traces permit failure diagnosis;
- exceptions and missing functionality have named owners.

**Entry states:** ready; conditional entry with a documented non-critical exception and authorised risk acceptance; or not ready when a critical dependency is missing. Automate these checks where practical.

For a fuller treatment of this specific failure mode, see [Starting SIT Is Not the Same as Being Ready for SIT](./2026-09-03).

### 3. Shared system-quality evidence

Aggregate existing CI/CD and operational signals rather than asking each team to write another status report. A useful view shows:

- contract and integration compatibility by dependency;
- critical journey verification and last successful run;
- environment/test-data blockers and their age;
- unresolved cross-team defects, owners and impact;
- release exceptions and explicit acceptance decisions;
- production SLOs, incidents and regression signals.

Every red condition needs an owner and decision path. An unowned “blocked by another team” ticket is a governance failure, not a valid long-term status.

## Release assurance without a universal QA sign-off

Adopt **risk-based system readiness assessment**:

1. **Independent low-risk change:** service-local tests and contractual checks run automatically; a team deploys within established guardrails.
2. **Material interface or data change:** provider/consumer compatibility and selected integration flows provide additional evidence.
3. **Coordinated high-risk release:** a named release/system owner assesses critical journeys, operational readiness, security/reliability risks, unresolved defects and rollback options.
4. **Exception:** the authorised decision-maker accepts or rejects a clearly described residual risk. QE documents its assessment without silently owning the decision.

A deployment should be observable and recoverable, not merely test-green. Google's [SRE workbook on implementing SLOs](https://sre.google/workbook/implementing-slos/) provides a basis for service-level reliability goals and error-budget decisions. Trace propagation across services improves diagnosis; see [OpenTelemetry Context Propagation](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/concepts/context-propagation/).

## Measurements that reveal systemic risk

Avoid treating test counts, pass percentages or pre-project “expected defect rates” as measures of overall quality. Prioritise actionable signals:

| Signal | What it can reveal | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cross-team integration failure frequency | Unstable service boundaries | Normalise by change volume and severity |
| Time from change to compatibility feedback | How early contracts detect failures | Separate flaky checks from real incompatibility |
| Percentage of changes independently testable/deployable | Architectural coupling and coordination cost | Not every regulated system can deploy independently |
| SIT entry failures / blocked time | Weak readiness controls and environment dependencies | An initial rise may mean improved detection |
| Escaped incidents affecting critical journeys | Missed system-level risks | Low volumes are statistically noisy |
| Change failure and recovery time | Production impact and resilience | Interpret within change type and service criticality |
| SLO attainment / error-budget burn | Actual user-facing reliability | Requires sound service-level objectives |

DORA provides a [loosely coupled teams capability](https://dora.dev/capabilities/loosely-coupled-teams/) and [continuous integration guidance](https://dora.dev/capabilities/continuous-integration/) that can inform assessment, but none of these metrics alone proves a particular QE operating model caused an outcome.

## Practical implementation

A sensible pilot involves **one critical business journey**, not an organisation-wide QA reorganisation.

**First two weeks — map accountability.** Identify journey owner, participating services, contracts, missing controls and current failure-detection points. Document who can accept risks and unblock dependencies.

**Next three weeks — shift checks left.** Implement version compatibility checks for the highest-risk interface changes, agree minimum readiness evidence and make CI results available to relevant teams.

**Next three weeks — close system-level gaps.** Automate a small number of critical end-to-end checks and cross-service negative scenarios; link failure diagnostics and create a shared exception view.

**Continuous — adapt the model.** Review incidents, coordination delays, testing bottlenecks and the cost of shared environments. Move checks into team-owned pipelines when they can reliably replace late-stage verification.

This is an illustrative implementation sequence, **not an evidence-backed universal eight-week delivery estimate**.

## Risks and trade-offs

- **A central QE bottleneck may reappear.** Avoid mandatory approvals for ordinary changes; reserve human assessment for defined risk thresholds.
- **Teams may optimise local green status.** Assign explicit owners to critical journeys and their production outcomes.
- **Contract tests can create false confidence.** They establish selected compatibility properties, not complete semantic or business correctness.
- **Shared integrated environments are costly and flaky.** Reduce dependence through isolated tests and version-aware contracts; retain realistic system checks where needed.
- **Strict consistency can suppress autonomy.** Standardise evidence, critical controls and ownership, not every team's test framework or implementation details.
- **Metrics can be gamed or misread.** Use trends and incident learning for improvement, not simple inter-team league tables.
- **Legacy coupling and regulatory rules may limit autonomy.** In those cases, use stronger coordinated release controls while reducing architectural coupling over time.

## Key takeaways

1. **Quality belongs to delivery teams; system-level risk must also have a named owner.**
2. **The most important gap is often between teams, not within a test suite.**
3. **Automated boundary checks and early integration reduce dependence on late SIT.**
4. **Central QE should enable consistency, diagnose systemic risk and challenge evidence—not execute or sign off everything.**
5. **Release readiness is a risk decision informed by test and operational evidence.**
6. **Judge improvement by independent testability, earlier feedback and fewer user-facing failures, not by the number of tests.**
